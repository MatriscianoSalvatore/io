DevFest Milano 2026

Saturday 10 October 2026, Randstad Box, Via San Vigilio. My third DevFest Milano in this building, and the first one from the front of the room: at half eleven, in the Coworking track, Stefano Soriano and I gave AI On-Device — Cooking from your fridge with Gemini Nano, MobileCLIP and Gemma, the story of AI Pantry, the Android app we built to look inside your fridge and suggest what to cook tonight, without a single photo ever leaving the phone.

Around it, a whole day of DevFest Milano, put on as every year since 2024 by GDG Milano and GDG Cloud Milano together, and with one theme this time: the year AI stops just answering and starts acting. Four tracks in parallel from nine to a quarter past six — Nexus, Coworking, Workshop, and a Secret Track held around one long meeting table — a handwritten sign pointing the way to all four, a neon sign in a red window nook, and a LEGO minifigure with the Duomo on its t-shirt.

The talk opens with a question: what happens when you decide to run all of your app's AI on the phone itself? No cloud, no API keys, no photos of your users' kitchens sitting on somebody's server. The answer is AI Pantry — you photograph the fridge and the pantry, the app recognises the ingredients, remembers what you have and writes a recipe out of it — and the talk is the honest version of how it got built: not just the architecture that works, but the two that did not, and what each of them cost.

Me, a couple of minutes in

From the second slide to the third: the question, and the app that answers it — scan the fridge, get dinner, 100% on-device.

I took the first half: why on-device at all — privacy before anything else, since no server ever sees the photo, there is no data processing agreement to sign, no retention policy to worry about and no log to leak; then resilience and cost, because a fridge is often where the signal is worst and every cloud call is paid for again by every user, every day — and chapter one, Gemini Nano. Stefano took it from Gemma onwards.

Five chapters and a bonus

  1. Gemini Nano, via ML Kit GenAI — the obvious choice: already in the operating system, multimodal, one Gradle dependency and zero megabytes in the APK. Except that it runs only on the latest flagships, not on the phones most people actually own. So: an upgrade, never the base, and checkStatus() before every single call.
  2. One model to rule them all: Gemma 4 E2B — bring your own open model and ask it both to look at the fridge and to write the recipes. It worked: correct ingredients, valid JSON, real recipes. It also took 5–10 minutes per scan on a Pixel 7, because a photo becomes hundreds of image tokens to prefill before the first word, and then every word is decoded one token at a time through two billion parameters.
  3. Zero-shot detection with MobileCLIP-S2 — recognising ingredients does not need a model that writes, it needs one that compares. "Is this an orange?" becomes a cosine similarity, and the classifier becomes a text file of hand-picked labels. Same phone, same photo of the fridge: minutes → seconds.
  4. Where the LLM earns its place — Gemma comes back for the one job no vocabulary can do, writing the recipes, text only this time: two-stage prompting, defensive JSON parsing with silent retries, 15–25 tokens a second.
  5. Distribution — every model travels differently: Gemini Nano costs zero megabytes, MobileCLIP's ~140 MB ride inside the APK, and Gemma's 2.6 GB go through Play for On-device AI as AI packs.
  6. Bonus, after the credits: EmbeddingGemma 2 — Google DeepMind's new on-device embedding model, released four days earlier and built on Gemma 4, open under Apache 2.0, shown on the app's hidden page where every task's model can be picked by hand.

The closing slide fitted in one line: before asking how to make a model faster, ask whether it is the right model.

The morning, in four rooms

Half nine, four rooms at once. Tomas Piaggio opened the Coworking room with multimodal RAG on Gemini and Firestore, "oltre il testo"; in Nexus, Matteo Gazzurelli asked what is left for developers when agents write the code — and his slide for the answer was the specification. The Secret Track had no stage at all, just a meeting table with laptops open on it, for Fabio Biondi on generative UI. Before the coffee break, Alfredo Morresi from Developer Relations at Google had a team of AI minions taking orders over Discord, and Balazs Horvath reported on giving AI twelve weeks to build the perfect employee. At half eleven, while we were starting, the Secret Track was on bug bashes and the workshop room was designing AI agents like Ford's assembly line; right after us, in our room, came a talk on becoming a founding engineer.

The afternoon: agents, everywhere

After lunch the agents got specific. Emmanuele Villa took an Android app from prompts to production with skills and MCP tools, with NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day as the API to build against; Andrea Alberto did agentic commerce with Milano Centrale as the proving ground; Francesco Montelli put a natural-language interface over an observability stack, on the grounds that an incident does not speak PromQL. Then the security end of it — Seif on managing AI security posture with the Secure AI Framework, and AURA, a risk framework for autonomous agents whose answer to an action nobody should take blindly is a box that says HOLD — and to close, a talk on why protocols are the real infrastructure of AI, while the workshop room built AI-native web apps until a quarter past six.
